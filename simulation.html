Wiring flow — the lit lane is the one running
  • The panel charges the pack; the pack runs the machine. Panel → charge controller → pack → buck → Uno. A bare panel swings from 0 to 22 V with every cloud — nothing digital lives on that.
  • 5 V, and nothing more, reaches the Arduino. Whatever the source, the board is fed 5 V — from the adapter over USB, or from the buck into the 5 V pin. The 14.8 V pack and the 18 V panel stop at the converter and never go past it.
  • Set the buck to 5.00 V before it ever touches the board. Turn the LM2596 pot with a meter on its output and nothing else connected, confirm 5.00 V, then connect. Feeding the 5 V pin bypasses the onboard regulator and the polarity diode, so a mis-set pot or a reversed lead kills the board with no second chance.
  • Servos on their own rail. Two servos stall at over an amp each. A second buck at 6 V, 3 A feeds them; only the signal wires go to D5 and D6. Sharing the Uno’s 5 V pin is how the board resets mid-turn.
  • One ground. Pack −, buck −, servo rail −, sensor blue − and Uno GND all meet on one rail. Every signal is measured against it.
  • Fuse the pack. A 5 A blade fuse on the pack + lead, before anything else. A shorted 4S pack will set fire to a jumper.
  • Selector, not a diode-OR. One source at a time into the module. The switch means the adapter can stay plugged in as the backup while you run on the pack.
  • Never the panel into the jack. 22 V open-circuit is over the Uno’s 20 V absolute maximum, and the current collapses the moment a servo moves.
  • Never 14.8 V into VIN or the barrel jack. The onboard regulator is rated 7–12 V; at 14.8 V it cooks under load. That is what the buck is for, and it is why the buck is set to 5 V and taken to the 5 V pin rather than back through the regulator.
  • Never 5 V into the barrel jack. The jack goes through the regulator, which needs about 6.5 V in to give 5 V out. Feed the jack 5 V and the board runs at roughly 4 V and behaves strangely. 5 V goes to the USB port or the 5 V pin, never the jack.
  • Never bypass the BMS. It is the only thing that stops a cell going under 3.0 V, and it is why the machine goes dark at the cut-off instead of the pack dying quietly.

  • Only the black core off a head goes to a pin. The brown is +14.8 V and the blue is 0 V. An LJ12A3 wants 6–36 V to run and switches its output to 0 V when it sees its material — that switched core is the only one the Uno ever touches.
  • D10 is INPUT_PULLUP, so the button goes to ground. One side to D10, the other to 0 V, and no resistor anywhere. The pin sits HIGH on the Uno’s own pull-up and reads LOW while the button is held.
  • The servos take signal only from D5 and D6. Their red goes to the 3 A rail and their brown to the common ground. Two of them stalling is over an amp, which is more than the board’s 5 V pin will pass.
  • A3 is a pin like any other. Using an analog pin as a digital output is legal and deliberate — the digital ones were full. It is worth saying so before a panelist asks whether it was a mistake.
  • The HC-SR04 is a 5 V part. It sits on the board rail, not on the 14.8 V head rail. Trig is an output, echo is an input, and pulseIn blocks until it hears back.
  • Grommets and strain relief. Every lead enters the box through a grommet and the loom is zip-tied under the throat, so nothing hangs into the mechanism and no core is ever taking the weight of its own cable.
  • D13 is not a free pin. It also drives the Uno’s own on-board LED, so the metal light and the board light are the same thing. Live with it, or move the metal LED and say why.
  • Never the barrel jack. 5 V through it leaves the board running at about 4 V; 14.8 V through it cooks the regulator. 5 V goes to the USB port or to the 5 V pin, and to nothing else.
  • A relay changeover needs its two extra parts. A flyback diode across the coil, or the transistor driving it dies; and a reservoir capacitor across the output, or the board resets every time the contact breaks on its way over.

      Why the sensors sit where they do

      the design logic

      Stage 1 — the two proximity heads, up on the wooden blocks

      The capacitive head reacts to solid plastic — a Coke bottle, a C2 bottle — and much more strongly when there is still liquid inside, because it senses the change in the electric field and water changes it a lot. It also reacts to metal. The inductive head reacts to metal and nothing else.

      That difference is the whole trick: capacitive alone means plastic; the inductive joining in means metal. The sketch reads the inductive pin first, so when both go LOW the answer is Metal Waste.

      Why the same height. Both heads face each other across the throat on the same line, so one object crossing that line reaches both at the same instant. If one sat higher, a short can could reach the upper head and not the lower one, and the answer would depend on how deep you happened to push it.

      Why the gap is set the way it is. Too close together and the two heads share a target — anything passing between them trips both, and both‑high reads as metal, so a plastic bottle would be called metal. Too far apart and a small can slips down the middle without either head seeing it. The spacing is chosen so a normal can or bottle fills the gap and a hand can still hold it there.

      Why they are above the IR. They are the opening stage of detection — waste passes them on the way in, before it reaches anything else.

      Stage 2 — the IR at the bottom of the hole, just above the tap

      Bio waste is the awkward one. Paper and rotten fruit are soft, light and irregular — they go straight through the hole and fall. There is nothing solid to hold up to a proximity head, and a proximity head would not reliably see them anyway.

      So the IR sits at the bottom of the hole, above the tap, aimed across the face of the tap. The tap is closed, it catches whatever fell, and the IR is looking at exactly that spot. Anything that drops in ends up in the IR's field of view and stays there until the tap opens.

      The catch. An IR reflective sensor sees any object in front of it, not only bio waste. So the IR is really "something landed on the tap", and it is bio only by convention. That is why it is the last thing checked — and why a bottle or a can that skipped stage 1 gets called bio.

      Where the two stages meet. A tall item resting on the tap can still reach up into stage 1 — a standing bottle does exactly that. Then both stages report at once, and because the bio branch in the sketch has no guard it runs last and takes over the display. Try the plastic bottle with Drop straight in and watch the LCD.

      The turntable is three parts, not one

      From the bottom up: a rectangular plywood support box on the floor, a fixed triangular base plate screwed down onto it, three castors near that plate's corners, and only then the rotating triangular plate with the bins riding on those castors.

      Why not just bolt the plate to the servo. A servo's output shaft is a drive, not a bearing. Three loaded bins sit about 67 mm off the axis, so their weight becomes a bending moment on that shaft: the plate droops on the loaded side, see-saws as it swings round, and the gearbox wears out. The castors carry that weight straight down into the box and leave the servo doing nothing but turning — which is all a 270° hobby servo is good for.

      Why the base is a triangle too. Three castors under the three corners put a support directly beneath each bin, which is where the load is, without cutting a full disc of plywood. And because the base does not turn, its corners stay pointing the same way while the top plate rotates over them — you can see that in the scene.

      Why a rectangle underneath. A box is the easy shape to cut square and glue rigid, its flat footprint stops the whole tower rocking when a bin swings past, and the hollow gives you somewhere to mount the servo and the D10 button out of the way.

      Why both servos are 270°

      travel on D5, torque on D6

      Both tap_servo (D5) and tap_servo1 (D6) are 270° metal‑gear digital servos, not the 9 g SG90 that comes in most kits. They are the same part for two different reasons — the platform needs the travel, the gate needs the torque — and a panel will ask about the cost, so the numbers are worth knowing.

      D5 — the platform: it is a travel problem

      Three bins take turns under one hole, so their parked positions are spread round the plate. From the sketch: bio write(0) = 0°, metal write(80) = 120°, plastic write(165) = 247.5°. The furthest bin is nearly 250° from zero.

      So an SG90 cannot do it. It stops at 180°. It would park bio and metal and then stall against its own end stop trying to reach plastic — a two‑bin machine.

      “Then space the bins 90° apart.” They will not fit. The bins ride 67 mm off the axis and are about 120 mm across. At 120° apart their centres are 2 × 67 × sin 60° = 116 mm apart — they only just clear. At 90° apart it is 2 × 67 × sin 45° = 95 mm, and they touch. Three bins that size need the whole circle, and the whole circle needs at least 240° of sweep. 270° is the standard size above that.

      Then the load, second. The castors carry the weight straight down, so the servo never holds the plate up — but it still starts and stops three filling bins, and every stop lets them keep going and twist the shaft back. That is what the metal gearbox is for. Digital is for the parking: a faster correction loop holds the bin square under the hole instead of settling a few degrees off, and a few degrees off at the rim is waste hitting the bin lip.

      D6 — the gate: it is a torque problem

      What the gate is actually for. It is the floor of the hole. Everything dropped in lands on it and stays there — held on the stage‑1 sensor line while the sketch reads it, held while the plate turns underneath — and it opens only once the correct bin has arrived. Without it, waste falls straight through into whichever bin is parked and nothing is sorted. It is a stop first and a door second.

      Travel is not the argument here. A flap only has to swing clear, ~90°, so an SG90 has the sweep. What decides it is how much it can hold, and that is set by the arm:

      torque needed = weight × distance from the hinge

      Flap armSG90 · 1.8 kg·cm270° MG · 10 kg·cm
      4 cm450 g at the tip2.5 kg
      6 cm300 g at the tip1.6 kg
      8 cm225 g at the tip1.2 kg

      Those are stall figures with nothing spare — a servo held near its rating sags, buzzes and answers late, which is exactly the “not responsive when it is heavy” behaviour. Substitute your own servo's rated kg·cm from its datasheet; the shape of the table does not change.

      And it gets landed on. Every item is dropped from the mouth onto a closed flap, so the gearbox takes an impact, not a steady load. Nylon gears round off; metal ones do not.

      So: the same 270° metal‑gear digital servo on D6. Not for the sweep — for the holding torque, for a gearbox that survives being landed on, and so the machine carries one spare part instead of two.

      The design rule that follows: lengthen the flap to cover a wider hole and the torque you need rises in the same proportion. That is why the flap is no longer than the bore.

      One thing to own before they find it

      The sketch drives the gate from write(180) closed to write(0) open. On a 270° unit that is a full 270° swing — far more than a gate needs, and time paid on every single drop. The flap only needs about 90°, which on a 270° servo is write(120) → write(60). Say it before the panel says it: we know, it is on the improvements list, and it does not affect where the waste lands — only how long the cycle takes.

      Tips & strategy for the defense

      know the weakness, own it

      Know the weaknesses

      W1

      One piece of waste at a time

      Two pieces means two materials and one answer. A can and a bottle together will trip the inductive head and the whole load goes to the metal bin. Never load a handful — feed it one item, wait for the platform to come home, then feed the next.

      W2

      Anything on the tap reads as bio

      The IR sees any object, so an item that never registered at stage 1 is called bio whatever it is made of. Throw a can in fast and it lands in the bio bin — not a wiring fault, just the loop looking twice a second while the can falls past stage 1 in about a tenth of a second.

      W3

      Room light alone can trigger bio — with nothing in the machine

      The bio head is an IR obstacle module: an IR LED, a receiver and an LM393 comparator with a trim pot. It cannot tell its own emitter's light from the room's. Direct sun, a halogen or incandescent lamp, or a camera flash into the throat pushes the receiver past the threshold — the module's own output LED lights, D7 reads LOW, and handleWasteDetection() calls it bio: green LED on A3, platform to 0, gate open, on an empty tap. Keep the throat shaded. Hood the feed hole, do not set the prototype by a window or under a spotlight, and tune the pot in the room you will present in. Ordinary LED or fluorescent ceiling light emits little IR and is usually fine; sunlight and hot-filament lamps are what break it. (Note it is one digital bit, not a signal level — the pot sets where the line falls, and the module's LED just mirrors that bit.)

      W4

      Wet bio can read as plastic

      The capacitive head responds to water. Hold a very wet fruit up at stage 1 and it can trip as plastic. Bio waste should be dropped, never presented.

      W5

      Bin-full mostly watches one bin

      Home is write(90) and metal is write(80), so at rest the ultrasonic is pointed into the metal bin. Empty all three before you start rather than trusting the alarm.

      Technique on the day

      1

      Bio — just drop it in

      Paper, a crumpled ball, a piece of fruit: put it in the hole and let it fall. It lands on the tap, the IR picks it up, green LED, bio bin. Do not hold it near the proximity heads.

      2

      Plastic — present it, don't throw it

      Hold the bottle into the mouth so its body sits between the two proximity heads. Wait for the LCD to read Plastic Waste and the yellow LED to light, then let go. The platform is already turning by then, so it drops into the right bin.

      3

      Metal — same, slowly

      Feed the can in slowly so both heads see it together. Both go LOW, the sketch checks the inductive first, the LCD reads Metal Waste. Say this out loud while you do it — it is the clearest moment to explain the two-sensor logic.

      4

      Let it finish before the next piece

      One pass takes about 1.7 s: 1 s for the platform, 0.2 s for the tap, then delay(500). Wait for the LCD to return to Automatic Waste / Segregation before the next item.

      5

      Demo order, and own the limitation

      Name the two stages before you touch anything, then run bio → plastic → metal. If you are confident, finish by deliberately throwing the can in so it lands in the bio bin, and explain why. A panel that hears you predict the failure trusts the rest of the work far more than one that finds it for you.

      6

      Tune the IR in the room, not at home

      Power up with the hopper empty and look at the green LED and the module's own output LED. If either is lit with nothing on the tap, the room's IR is already over your threshold — back the trim pot off until it goes out, then check that a sheet of paper on the tap still trips it. Do this again if the blinds get opened or someone points a light at you.

      7

      Before you present

      Empty all three bins, bring a spare of each item, check the 14.8 V pack, and leave the serial monitor open — the running Distance: line is easy proof the ultrasonic is alive if a panelist asks.

      Analysis of your sketch

      ten, by severity
      1

      Bio is not guarded, so it overrides metal and plastic

      The metal branch has no guard, plastic has && !metalDetected, but bio has none at all. A tin can that also reflects the IR sensor runs moveServos(80,0) and then moveServos(0,0) — the platform swings to metal, then to bio, and the can lands in the bio bin. Add && !metalDetected && !plasticDetected to the bio branch.

      2

      Only the metal bin is watched between drops

      Home is tap_servo.write(90) and metal is 80 — nearly the same angle. So at rest the ultrasonic looks into the metal bin. Bio and plastic are only checked in the one pass right after they receive waste, then the platform returns home. A bin can sit full and unreported.

      3

      Sensor pins have no pull-up

      pinMode(..., INPUT) leaves the pin floating when an NPN open-collector sensor is not conducting, so it can read LOW at random. Use INPUT_PULLUP (or fit external pull-ups) on pins 3, 4 and 7.

      4

      pulseIn has no timeout

      If the echo never comes back, pulseIn(echoPin, HIGH) blocks for a full second, every pass. Use pulseIn(echoPin, HIGH, 25000) and treat 0 as "far away".

      5

      A no-echo reading looks like a full bin

      duration of 0 gives distance of 0, which passes distance <= 20 and fires the alarm. Guard with if (distance > 0 && distance <= 20).

      6

      lcd.init() runs every idle pass

      resetServosAndLCD() is called from handleWasteDetection() whenever nothing is detected — twice a second — and it re-initialises the display each time. Move lcd.init() and lcd.backlight() into setup().

      7

      1 s may not cover a 247° swing

      These are 270° servos, so write(165) is about 247° of real travel. Packing the bins corner to corner on a triangular plate cuts the inertia the servo has to fight, but the travel is unchanged — and if the gate opens before the plate arrives, the waste lands in the wrong bin. Time the worst case (home → plastic) and raise the delay(1000) if needed.

      8

      Unrecognised waste jams the machine silently

      If nothing reads LOW, handleWasteDetection() falls through to resetServosAndLCD(): the gate stays closed at 180 and the LCD keeps showing the idle message, while the waste sits in the throat forever. Try the glass shard — nothing happens and nothing tells you why. A "not recognised" message plus a timeout would fix it. (Click the stuck waste to take it back out.)

      9

      Locked-out loop free-runs

      When detectionMaintenance() returns true the loop returns before delay(500), so it spins as fast as the serial print allows. Harmless, but it floods the serial monitor — you can see it in the panel above.

      10

      The gate is left open when a bin fills

      The full-bin branch sounds the alarm, prints, clears operationsEnabled and returns true — and never touches tap_servo1. The trip fires on the pass straight after a drop, when the gate is still at write(0), so the machine locks out with the floor of the throat hanging open. The sorting stops; the hole does not. Anything dropped in while the buzzer is going falls straight through into the bin that was just declared full, and the gate servo holds at 0 under load for the whole alarm. resetServosAndLCD() is the only thing that closes it, and that does not run until D10 is pressed. One line fixes it — tap_servo1.write(180); before the return true.

      Try it: fill a bin, and while the alarm is going, feed another item. It goes in.
